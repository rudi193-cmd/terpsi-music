# The Nine O'Clock Shrimp

*Fiction. Not canonical — governs nothing, reconciles nothing. An easter egg, left on the table with the tip.*

The place had good light and a table near the outlet, which told me the woman running it knew her regulars. She took my coat and my hat at the door — the hat was a rental, and I said so, and she handled it like it wasn't — and walked me back like I was somebody.

"Can I get you a drink or a menu?"

"Whatever's warm and has caffeine in it," I said. "And tell me what's good tonight."

She glanced at the clock over the register. It was pushing nine.

"Stay away from the shrimp."

You learn a lot about an establishment in the first two minutes. Most places would've let me order the shrimp and learned nothing from it. This one told me the truth at the cost of a sale, and that's the kind of place a fellow comes back to. I ordered pie instead, and she brought me both kinds — the last slice of apple crumble and the last of the key lime, on the sensible grounds that end-of-day pie shouldn't be made to choose which of itself survives. The coffee came black, but she left room at the top.

I never use the room. It's just nice to have. Freedom you won't exercise but appreciate — a lot of good design comes down to that.

I had a plan for the pies. Crumble first, for stability. Citrus held in reserve, for when the evening needed sharpening.

The evening sharpened early.

He came in with the rain, which hadn't existed until he needed it, and sat down across from me without a word — a big man in a trench coat, in July — and put his fork into my key lime like he'd ordered it in a previous life and was only now collecting.

"You can call me Robert," he said, around the pie. "Thanks for this. Haven't had anything solid since last night. Liquids aplenty, though. Brown ones, at least."

I slid him the crumble too. A man running on brown liquids needs ballast more than I need dessert symmetry, and besides, you can't interrogate a fellow properly while he's hungry. It skews the results.

"I was told to meet someone here tonight," he said. "And seeing as you're the only one here, I guess it must be you."

"Told by whom?"

"A little birdie." He wiped his mouth. "Word is something's being built here that my employer might not like."

I'll tell you what I told him, free of charge, since he'd had my pie. Something was being built there, all right. A roster. A library. An attendance sheet. A ledger. Band programs — kids and their tubas, permission slips and fee waivers and who's got a nut allergy on the bus to regionals. The most boring treasure in the world, which is to say: the kind worth guarding properly.

Because the thing being built was mostly locks. Every kid in their own lane, sealed, from the first line written about them. Nothing leaves the building without an announcement. No machine gets the last word — a named human seals every answer or it stays a draft forever. And nobody, no matter what they're holding, gets to keep a standing score on a person and carry it door to door. If your business model is knowing things about children you haven't got a named, dated, signed reason to know, you're not going to like the establishment. That's rather the point of it.

"Who's the employer, Robert?"

"Don't know exactly. I work through envelopes." He shrugged, a mountain range relocating. "But they've got deep pockets. I've heard whisperings. Names you'd know. The big pots."

The attention merchants. The everything-about-everyone-in-one-pot school of cooking. I didn't hang anything on thirdhand birdsong — could be them, could be somebody wearing their shadow; every two-bit operator in town claims deep pockets are backing him, it makes the envelopes feel heavier. But the shape of it held. A band program in one town is nothing to a big pot. The *pattern* is something else. If small rooms full of sensitive things start locking their own doors and doing their own thinking in their own basements — well. That generalizes. That's the whispering worth whispering about.

We might have gotten somewhere ugly if the waitress hadn't come back.

"Sounds like you two have a little conflict," she said, refilling nothing, in the way of a woman who has raised children and knows a schoolyard when she sees one. "Why don't you do what my kids do at band camp. See who can sing farthest in a song."

I want it on the record that this was better dispute resolution than most I've seen deployed professionally. Cheaper than lawyers. Better audit trail — everybody in the room hears exactly who folded, and when.

I went old, because the songbook I know best I'm not permitted to perform, and a man should honor his handicaps. Clementine. The pre-envelope catalog, the songs nobody's pockets are deep enough to own. I got as far as the cavern and the canyon and I was feeling good about my position.

Then Robert sang.

Not bar-singing. Not envelope-courier singing. The real kind, carried a long way and set down gently — an old verse about who'd be your lover after he was gone, whether it'd be the moon that heard your sighing. The rain leaned in to listen. The coffee machine held its steam.

And in the middle of it, sweet and casual as you please, he sang the word *willow*.

Now — probably nothing. Old songs are full of willows. They're weepy trees; it's practically their job. But in my line of work that particular word stays in the back of the house with the rest of the machinery nouns, where no customer ever hears it. And here was the envelope man, singing it across my table the same night he'd mentioned deep pockets and birdies at windows.

"Which is it, Robert? Coincidence and a good ear — or is somebody letting me know how far through the window they've seen?"

He set down his fork. And the whole picture rearranged itself.

"Maybe the birdie sent me to warn you," he said. "Not to watch you."

It fit. It fit better than I liked. A watcher takes the booth by the door and leaves no impression. This man had walked in loud, eaten my pie, named the pockets, and sung the back-of-house word to my face. Every move he'd made all evening was a knock on the glass.

"Then knock properly. What's the warning?"

"If there's a gap they can exploit to shut this down," Robert said, "they will."

And here is where I did something that must have puzzled him, because I smiled. The tired, genuine smile of a man handed a threat he's already read.

I opened the briefcase and set the document on the table, turned it to face him. The establishment's dirty secret: *we keep a list of the gaps.* Written down. Numbered. Right at the front of the file where you can't miss it — *read this first,* it says — the full ledger of what's unfinished, unverified, and unproven, sitting in the open. There's even a note warning readers not to trust our own inventory of what's built. *Go check,* it says. *Treat it as a claim, not a fact.*

Because the gap you've named is a door you're watching. It's the gap you've papered over that they come through — the count you quoted but never derived, the guard you never once tried to break. So every lock in the place has to prove it can fail before anyone's allowed to call it a lock. We attempt the forbidden thing on purpose, regularly, and file the refusal.

We are, professionally speaking, our own first burglars.

"Take a message back through the window," I told him, standing, settling the rental hat just so. "Tell the birdie: message received, and thank you kindly. They're welcome to hunt for gaps. Truly. Every one they find and we close makes the place harder for the next one. They think they're casing the joint. From where I sit, they're doing free inspections."

I left bills on the table — the check, the double tip, and Robert's pie, as promised. The waitress had earned the double the moment she steered me off the shrimp; that's a professional, and there's a code about professionals.

"You're alright, Robert. Eat something solid tomorrow. If another envelope comes, you know which table I'll be at."

At the door I touched the hat brim to the woman who ran the place.

"The pie was exceptional. The counsel better. I'll be back — but next time I'm trying the shrimp at a respectable hour."

And out into the rain, which by then had decided to stay.
